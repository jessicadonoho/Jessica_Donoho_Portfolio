ctrl^shift: algorithms aren't working for us

Social media algorithms push people toward doomscrolling and one-sided information, and get in the way of what they actually opened the app to do. ctrl^shift is a companion app that lets people see past their algorithm and decide how they spend their time.

01 · overview

About the project

tl;dr

Social media users, especially on Instagram, need a way to take back control of how they use it. Current algorithms interfere with their goals and can lead to doomscrolling and biased information. That affects how they talk to the people they know and how they see the world every day.

How might we help social media users see content that their personalized algorithms won't show them?

03 · interviews

What people told me about their feeds

I ran three semi-structured interviews after testing my questions in two mock interviews. Two were by phone and one over Zoom. Every participant actively used social media and knew platforms like Instagram, Reddit, X, YouTube, and Bluesky.

finding 1

Connection is the core need

Every interviewee said staying connected with people they know is why they stay, not the content.

finding 2

Control feels impossible

"You can't really control it." The algorithm shows what it wants, overriding what people intended.

finding 3

Habit loops resist change

"Muscle memory of opening Instagram." Even people who wanted to quit found themselves back without deciding to.

finding 4

Spreading out as a strategy

Tech-savvy users spread their use across platforms on purpose, so no single source controls what they see.

Unless I'm the one designing the algorithm, I can't trust this black box.

Interview participant, on algorithmic trust

Limitation: all participants knew Instagram well, so many examples centered on it. Two were also very aware of recommendation algorithms, which may have shaped their answers.

04 · personas

Two ways of living with the algorithm

I compared responses across participants to find patterns in behaviors, motivations, and frustrations, then turned them into two personas.

05 · user flow

Mapping the path before drawing screens

The user flow mapped each step from entry to completing core tasks. It showed me gaps and unnecessary steps early, so I could move to a paper prototype with confidence.

06 · design evolution

How the design found its shape

stage 01

Paper: a rotary dial

Groups sat in a circle, a nod to analog phones. Fast to iterate, but it wouldn't scale.

stage 02

Wireframe: to screen

The circle broke navigation, so groups became a vertical list and "Timer" became "Schedule."

stage 03

Hi-fi: visual language

A dark teal identity emerged, and onboarding was split into focused steps.

stage 04

Post-testing

Simplified scheduling logic and moved key features somewhere people could find them.

07 · paper prototype

Eight screens on a table

Before any digital wireframes, I drew an eight-screen paper prototype, played the wizard behind the curtain, and tested it with one user in an informal session.

08 · hi-fi & testing

Wireframes, hi-fi, and three rounds of testing

Creating the wireframe

Using the paper prototype as a blueprint, I built the wireframe in Figma at real screen sizes, with consistent spacing, realistic placeholder text, and linked frames. That step showed gaps the sketch had glossed over: elements competing for attention, and flows that worked on paper but broke once you had to click through them.

Iterating from testing

Where people hesitated, misclicked, or asked questions pointed straight to what needed to change. At low fidelity those fixes were quick. Participants sometimes struggled to engage with grey boxes, but that kept feedback on structure instead of looks.

Hi-fi and usability testing

In the hi-fi prototype, testing focused on algorithm customization and scheduling. The biggest issues were discoverability and clarity, especially the algorithm preference controls and vague terms like "session." Some patterns followed familiar social media conventions, but people still needed clearer guidance. I simplified interactions, improved labels, moved "User Analysis" to the home screen, and replaced "On This Topic" with clearer wording.

09 · final design

The final design, flow by flow

The overall flow didn't change much from the user flow to the hi-fi prototype. What changed was the detail: clearer wording, a longer onboarding, and features moved to where people looked for them.

Onboarding

Testing showed that people didn't understand key ideas when they ran into them in the app, so I split one long block of text into short steps: groups, scheduling, and goals. The goals people pick here shape their home screen.

hover a screen to zoom in

Home, settings, and tools

Before browsing, people have to pick a group, so the app never opens straight into a feed. Settings and tools sit at the bottom of the home screen, where they're easy to reach. Scheduling sets when and how long people can scroll. The schedule can change once a week, and there's an emergency stop that works three times a month.

hover a screen to zoom in

Groups

Groups are themed containers for parts of the feed. Friends is built around staying connected. News adds a topic filter. Hidden Content shows posts the algorithm usually keeps from you, and explains why each one was hidden.

hover a screen to zoom in

Discovery and reflection

Fact Check and Similar Posts sit under posts in most groups. Similar Posts shows matching and contrasting opinions together. I briefly split them apart after one tester said they didn't want to see opinions they disagreed with, but I went back to showing both, because seeing both sides is the point. When a session ends, people can log how they feel, and that mood shows up later in User Analysis.

10 · design system

A brand about shifting control

The style guide sets up ctrl^shift as a brand about shifting control from social media platforms back to the people using them. It defines the color ramp, type, and core components I used across the hi-fi prototype.

color

Dark teal to mint

Six steps from near-black through deep teal and sage to pale mint. Dark screens with light mint for actions and selected states.

type

Monospace throughout

One monospace family in bold and regular, which fits the keyboard-shortcut name.

components

A small, reusable kit

Group tiles, pill buttons, toggles, select menus, input cards, and icon buttons for Manage Algorithm, Settings, and Set Schedule.

mood

Tech in the garden

A moodboard of retro computers set in overgrown greenery and glowing green textures.

11 · features

What ctrl^shift does

Session scheduler

Set time limits and choose which days scrolling is allowed. Testing showed "session" wasn't immediately clear.

News group feed

Pulls Instagram and Bluesky posts on a topic, with a "Similar Posts" button to find other viewpoints. Renamed from "On This Topic."

Manage algorithm

Tag content preferences and see a chart of your engagement. "User Analysis" moved to the home screen after testing.

Hidden Content

A group of posts the algorithm usually keeps from you, each with a note explaining why it was hidden.

Session reflection

When a session ends, log how you feel. Moods show up later in User Analysis, because how people feel after scrolling is part of the problem, not just what they see.

13 · reflection

What I learned

lesson 1

Language is design

Many usability issues came from wording, not interaction. "Session," "On This Topic," "Include/Exclude": naming is a design decision.

lesson 2

Iterating in tension

A "same vs. contrasting opinion" split contradicted user autonomy. Building the hi-fi forced me to resolve that.

lesson 3

The problem evolves

The goal shifted from "escape the filter bubble" to "control your own social media experience," a change that only came from research.
